What is quantum optimal control?

Quantum optimal control asks how to shape the time-dependent fields that drive a quantum system — laser, microwave, radio-frequency or voltage pulses — so that it performs a task as well as possible: prepare a state, implement a gate, or sense a signal, within limits on time, power and bandwidth, and in the presence of noise.

The field grew out of nuclear magnetic resonance and the laser control of chemical reactions in the 1980s and 1990s. Today it is a core tool of quantum technologies, from superconducting and trapped-ion processors to atomic clocks, quantum simulators and sensors.

Optimal control and the circuit model

Quantum algorithms are usually written as circuits of fixed gates. Quantum optimal control works one level lower, designing the physical pulses themselves — for a single gate, a block of gates, or an entire task such as state preparation or sensing.

Designing pulses

Strengths

  • Uses the full hardware dynamics to approach the speed limit and replace multi-gate blocks with one pulse.
  • Handles leakage, crosstalk and line distortion directly in the design.
  • Applies beyond unitary gates: state preparation, sensing and open-system control.

Limitations

  • Needs an accurate device model or costly closed-loop calibration.
  • Pulses are hardware-specific, harder to benchmark, and scale exponentially with qubit count.

Composing gates

Strengths

  • Hardware-independent abstraction: calibrate a small gate set once and compose large circuits.
  • Error correction and randomized benchmarking rely on modular gates with simple error models.

Limitations

  • Decompositions into native gates are often much longer than the physics requires.
  • Fixed gate times spend coherence and hide hardware couplings a custom pulse could exploit.

Where the two meet: gate synthesis, pulse-level compilation and variational algorithms as control.
