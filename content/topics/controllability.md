Before asking *how* to steer a quantum system, one can ask *whether* it can be steered at all. **Controllability**
asks which states or operations are reachable with the available controls. For finite-dimensional systems the
answer is algebraic. The reachable set is determined by the **dynamical Lie algebra** generated by the drift and
control Hamiltonians. This is one of the cleanest results of the field, and the starting point for gate design,
for the analysis of [[control-landscapes|control landscapes]] and, more recently, for understanding variational quantum
algorithms.

## The setting

A closed system with controls entering linearly — a *bilinear* control system — evolves as

$$\dot U(t) = -i\Big(H_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} u_k(t)\,H_k\Big)\,U(t), \qquad U(0) = \mathbb 1 ,$$

with real control functions $u_k(t)$. The question is which unitaries $U(T)$ (or which states
$U(T)|\psi_0\rangle$) can be reached for some choice of controls and some time $T$.

## The Lie-algebra rank condition

Switching quickly between different Hamiltonians generates their commutators: to second order,
$e^{-iA\tau}e^{-iB\tau}e^{iA\tau}e^{iB\tau} \approx e^{-[A,B]\tau^2}$. Repeating the argument, the directions in
which the system can move are those of the **dynamical Lie algebra**

$$\mathfrak L = \mathrm{Lie}\big\{-iH_0,\,-iH_1,\,\dots,\,-iH_K\big\},$$

the smallest real vector space of anti-Hermitian matrices that contains the generators and is closed under
commutators. For finite-dimensional systems, the set of reachable unitaries is the connected Lie group
$e^{\mathfrak L}$ generated by this algebra. This rests on the theory of control systems on Lie groups
[@jurdjevic1972lie], and on the fact that unitary groups are compact, so the drift cannot push the system
irreversibly in one direction. In particular:

!!! note "Complete (operator) controllability"
    A $d$-level system is **completely controllable** — every unitary can be reached, up to a global phase — if and
    only if $\mathfrak L = \mathfrak{su}(d)$ or $\mathfrak u(d)$ [@ramakrishna1995controllability].

Computing $\mathfrak L$ is mechanical. One takes nested commutators of the generators and keeps the linearly
independent ones until the dimension stops growing; for full controllability it must reach $d^2-1$ (or $d^2$).
For specific classes of systems, sufficient conditions can be read from the structure of the coupling. For example,
a single control field driving several transitions at once can still give complete controllability of Morse- and
oscillator-like ladders [@schirmer2001complete].

## Different notions of controllability

What is "reachable" depends on what we want [@albertini2003notions]:

- **Operator (complete) controllability** — every unitary is reachable: $\mathfrak L \supseteq \mathfrak{su}(d)$.
  This is what [[gate-synthesis|gate synthesis]] needs.
- **Pure-state controllability** — every state can be reached from every other state. This is weaker: it also
  holds when $\mathfrak L$ is isomorphic to the symplectic algebra $\mathfrak{sp}(d/2)$ (with or without
  $\mathfrak u(1)$), which acts transitively on the sphere of states without containing all of $\mathfrak{su}(d)$.
- **Density-matrix (kinematic) controllability** — any two density matrices with the same spectrum are connected.
  For closed systems it is equivalent to operator controllability.

## Symmetries as obstructions

A conserved quantity blocks controllability. If an operator $S$ commutes with $H_0$ and with every $H_k$, then the
dynamics never mixes the eigenspaces of $S$ — for example, total spin or particle number in a symmetric system. The
converse is subtler: absence of symmetry gives a convenient *necessary* condition that is much easier to check than
the full Lie-algebra computation. A complete classification of the relevant subalgebras even yields a single
necessary and sufficient symmetry criterion [@zeier2011symmetry].

## Beyond finite, closed systems

- **Infinite dimensions.** For the Schrödinger equation in infinite-dimensional spaces (oscillators, particles
  in potentials), exact controllability generally fails for bilinear systems [@ball1982bilinear]. The relevant notion is
  then *approximate* controllability — reaching any target to arbitrary precision — studied since the earliest work on
  quantum controllability [@huang1983controllability]. See [[bosonic-control]].
- **Open systems.** With dissipation, the dynamics is no longer unitary and the notions change. Some states may not be
  reachable at all, while dissipation can also *help*, for example by driving the system into a target state
  [@altafini2012modeling]. See [[open-system-control]] and [[reservoir-engineering]].
- **Computability.** Whether a given task can be achieved with digitized controls is, in general, an undecidable
  question for a Turing machine [@bondar2020uncomputability]; see [[polynomial-optimization]].

## Why it matters in practice

- **Universality.** In quantum computing, controllability of a multi-qubit system is *universality*: almost any
  entangling two-qubit interaction combined with local controls suffices [@lloyd1995universal].
- **Necessary, not sufficient.** Controllability says a target can be reached in *some* time with *some* controls.
  How fast is the subject of [[quantum-speed-limits]] and [[geometric-control]], and with what resources is the
  subject of [[control-complexity]].
- **Landscapes.** Controllability is one of the assumptions under which control landscapes are free of traps; see
  [[control-landscapes]].
- **Variational algorithms.** The dynamical Lie algebra of a variational ansatz determines the scaling of its
  gradients, and therefore whether it suffers from barren plateaus [@larocca2022barren]; see
  [[variational-algorithms]].
